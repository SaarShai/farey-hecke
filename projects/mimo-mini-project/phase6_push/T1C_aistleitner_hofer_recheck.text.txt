## Task 2: Closely related papers I *can* speak to

These connections are based on mathematical content I can reason about:

**Franel–Landau (1924):** The foundational result. Let $F_Q$ be the Farey sequence of order $Q$, and define the discrepancy:
$$D(Q) = \sup_{0 \le \alpha \le \beta \le 1} \left|\#\{a/q \in F_Q : \alpha \le a/q \le \beta\} - J(Q)(\beta - \alpha)\right|$$

Then Franel proved (and Landau reformulated) that $D(Q) = O(Q^{1/2+\varepsilon})$ is equivalent to RH. The proof goes through an explicit identity connecting the discrepancy to sums of $\mu(n)$.

**Key identity (Franel):** For consecutive Farey fractions $a/q, a'/q'$ with $q' \le Q$,
$$\frac{a'}{q'} - \frac{a}{q} = \frac{1}{qq'}$$
and the "neighbor structure" of Farey fractions encodes the Möbius function via:
$$\sum_{\substack{q \le Q \\ (a,q)=1}} e^{2\pi i a n / q} = \sum_{d | n, d \le Q} d \cdot \mu(n/d)$$

This makes any statistic of consecutive Farey fractions intrinsically linked to $\mu$ and hence to $M(x) = \sum_{n \le x} \mu(n)$.

**Hooley (1970s–):** Studied the distribution of Farey fractions in short intervals using the circle method. His work on the "additive divisor problem" and related sieves touches on sums involving $\mu$ and $\varphi$ over Farey-type sets.

**Kanemitsu–Yoshimoto:** Studied weighted Farey discrepancy with the Riemann hypothesis connection, using explicit formulas. Their approach involves writing discrepancy-type sums as contour integrals involving $\zeta(s)$ and $\zeta(s)^{-1}$.

**Soundararajan:** His work on extreme values of $M(x)$ (and $|\zeta(1/2+it)|$) is relevant because pointwise behavior of $M(n)$ governs the fluctuations around any asymptotic formula. His paper "Moments of the Riemann zeta function" (2009) and related work on $M(x)$ bounds establish that $M(x)/\sqrt{x}$ has a certain distribution with tails governed by a specific probabilistic model.

## Task 4: The convergence of $\sum M(n)^2/n^3$ — a correction to your analysis

**Your analysis in point 4 contains an error.** You wrote:

> "Unconditionally, the bound is $M(n) = O(n)$ so $M^2/n^3 = O(1/n)$, divergent."

The bound $M(n) = O(n)$ is the trivial bound ($|\mu(k)| \le 1$). The **non-trivial unconditional bound** from the zero-free region of $\zeta(s)$ (Vinogradov–Korobov type) is:

$$M(n) = O\!\left(n \exp\!\left(-c \frac{(\log n)^{3/5}}{(\log\log n)^{1/5}}\right)\right)$$

This gives:
$$\frac{M(n)^2}{n^3} = O\!\left(n^{-1} \exp\!\left(-2c \frac{(\log n)^{3/5}}{(\log\log n)^{1/5}}\right)\right)$$

**The series $\sum M(n)^2/n^3$ converges unconditionally.** Here's the proof:

Substituting $u = \log n$ converts the sum to an integral:
$$\sum_{n} \frac{M(n)^2}{n^3} \;\ll\; \int_0^\infty \exp\!\left(-2c\, u^{3/5}(\log u)^{-1/5}\right) du$$

For large $u$, the integrand is bounded by $\exp(-c' u^{3/5})$, and:
$$\int_0^\infty e^{-c' u^{3/5}}\, du = \frac{5}{3(c')^{5/3}}\, \Gamma(5/3) < \infty$$

**Therefore:** $\sum M(n)^2/n^3$ converges absolutely and unconditionally. The convergence is not RH-equivalent; it follows from the zero-free region.

**What IS true:**
| Statement | Status |
|-----------|--------|
| $\sum M(n)^2/n^3$ converges | **Unconditionally true** |
| $\sum M(n)^2/n^3$ converges *quickly* (partial sums bounded by const + $O(N^{-1+\varepsilon})$) | Requires RH |
| $M(n) = O(n^{1/2+\varepsilon})$ | Equivalent to RH |
| $\sum M(n)^2/n^s$ converges for all $s > 1$ | Equivalent to RH (since under RH, $M(n)^2/n^s = O(n^{1+\varepsilon}/n^s)$, convergent for $s > 2$, and actually for $s > 1$ using more refined arguments under RH) |

**Analogy:** The prime number theorem says $\pi(x) \sim x/\log x$ (average). The Riemann formula $\pi(x) = \text{Li}(x) - \sum_\rho \text{Li}(x^\rho) + \ldots$ explains the fluctuations. Both are true; the second is strictly more informative.
