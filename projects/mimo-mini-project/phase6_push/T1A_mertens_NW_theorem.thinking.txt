   \frac{Q}{\Phi(Q)} = \frac{\pi^2}{3Q} + O\left(\frac{\log Q}{Q^2}\right).
   \]
   Substituting, we obtain
   \[
   NW_1(Q) = \frac{(1+M(Q))^2}{6Q} + O\left(\frac{(1+M(Q))^2 \log Q}{Q^2}\right).
   \]
   Under RH, \( M(Q) = O(Q^{1/2+\varepsilon}) \) for every ε>0. Therefore,
   \[
   NW_1(Q) = \frac{M(Q)^2}{6Q} + O\left(Q^{-1/2+\varepsilon}\right),
   \]
   since \( \frac{2M(Q)}{6Q} = O(Q^{-1/2+\varepsilon}) \) and \( \frac{1}{6Q} = O(Q^{-1}) \), and the error term from the approximation of \( \Phi(Q) \) is \( O(Q^{-1+2\varepsilon}\log Q) = o(Q^{-1/2+\varepsilon}) \). This step is rigorous under RH.

3. **Contribution from m≥2.** Let
   \[
   R(Q) = NW(Q) - NW_1(Q) = \frac{Q}{2\pi^2 \Phi(Q)} \sum_{m=2}^\infty \frac{|S_Q(m)|^2}{m^2}.
   \]
   We aim to show that \( R(Q) = C + O(Q^{-1/2+\varepsilon}) \). This is the hard part. We outline the heuristic argument and the required technical lemma.

   **Heuristic:** Assume that the values \( M(x) \) behave like independent random variables with mean 0 and variance x (Cramér model). Then for each m, the random variable \( S_Q(m) \) has mean 0 and variance
   \[
   \mathbb{E}[|S_Q(m)|^2] \sim Q \cdot \frac{\sigma(m)}{m},
   \]
   where \( \sigma(m) = \sum_{d\mid m} d \) is the sum-of-divisors function. Moreover, for different m, the \( S_Q(m) \) are approximately independent. Then the expected value of \( R(Q) \) is
   \[
   \mathbb{E}[R(Q)] \sim \frac{Q}{2\pi^2 \Phi(Q)} \sum_{m=2}^\infty \frac{Q \sigma(m)/m}{m^2} = \frac{Q^2}{2\pi^2 \Phi(Q)} \sum_{m=2}^\infty \frac{\sigma(m)}{m^3}.
   \]
   Using \( \Phi(Q) \sim \frac{3}{\pi^2} Q^2 \), this becomes
   \[
   \mathbb{E}[R(Q)] \sim \frac{1}{6} \sum_{m=2}^\infty \frac{\sigma(m)}{m^3}.
   \]
   The sum \( \sum_{m=1}^\infty \frac{\sigma(m)}{m^3} = \zeta(2)\zeta(3) \), so \( \sum_{m=2}^\infty \frac{\sigma(m)}{m^3} = \zeta(2)\zeta(3) - 1 \approx 0.977 \). Hence \( \mathbb{E}[R(Q)] \approx 0.1628 \), which is not equal to C. Therefore, the heuristic above is too crude.

   **Refined heuristic:** Perhaps the correct average is not with \( \sigma(m)/m \) but with a different multiplicative function. The constant C given is \( \frac{1}{2} \prod_p (1 + \frac{1}{p^2(p-1)}) \). This product expands as
   \[
   C = \frac{1}{2} \left(1 + \sum_{m=2}^\infty \frac{f(m)}{m^2}\right),
   \]
   for some multiplicative function f. Possibly, one can show that under a more refined model that accounts for correlations between \( M(Q/d) \) for different d, the average of \( |S_Q(m)|^2 \) is \( Q \cdot f(m) \) for some f, and then \( \sum_{m=2}^\infty f(m)/m^2 = 2C \). This would yield \( R(Q) \sim C \). The technical lemma needed is:
   \[
   \sum_{m=2}^\infty \frac{|S_Q(m)|^2}{m^2} = 2C \frac{\Phi(Q)}{Q} + O\left(\frac{\Phi(Q)}{Q} Q^{-1/2+\varepsilon}\right).
   \]
   Under RH, one might attempt to prove this by expanding \( |S_Q(m)|^2 \) as a double sum over divisors and swapping summations, as we did earlier, leading to an expression in terms of sums involving M(Q/d). Then one needs to estimate sums like \( \sum_{d=1}^Q \frac{M(Q/d)}{d} \) and \( \sum_{d,e=1}^Q \frac{d e\, M(Q/d) M(Q/e)}{\operatorname{lcm}(d,e)^2} \) with sufficient precision. Under RH, one expects that these sums are \( O(Q^{\varepsilon}) \) and that the main contribution comes from terms where d and e are large (close to Q) which are negligible. However, making this rigorous seems extremely difficult and likely requires assumptions beyond RH.
