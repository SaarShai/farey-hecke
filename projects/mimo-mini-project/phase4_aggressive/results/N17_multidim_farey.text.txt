### 1. Expected Energy $\langle E_N^2 \rangle$ in 2D: Hyperbolic vs. Euclidean
In 1D, the energy of the Farey sequence (often tied to the Heilbronn sum or logarithmic energy) relates to the Riemann Hypothesis and converges nicely when scaled by $N$. 

**Prediction:** In 2D, the naive Euclidean energy will **diverge** or fail to converge to a constant without a scale-dependent cutoff. However, if you switch to the **hyperbolic metric**, it will converge.
* **Why?** 2D Farey points are highly clustered near the axes (the $x=0, y=0$ planes) due to the $c$ denominator. Euclidean distances to the origin become vanishingly small, blowing up inverse-distance energy sums. 
* **The Fix:** The "natural" distance for Farey points is the distance in the hyperbolic half-space $\mathbb{H}^3$. In this geometry, the points $F_N^{(2)}$ behave much more uniformly. If you define energy using the Poincaré metric (or map the points via Ford sphere tangencies), the normalized $\langle E_N^2 \rangle$ will converge to a constant dictated by the spectral gap of the Laplacian on $SL(3, \mathbb{Z}) \backslash \mathbb{H}^3$.

### 2. Joint Density of 2D Gaps: The Level-Attraction Simplex
The iconic 1D BCZ result $f(x,y) = 2 \cdot \mathbf{1}_{x+y>1}$ represents *level attraction*: extreme gaps cannot be adjacent without a tiny gap separating them. 

**Prediction:** The 2D joint density will be supported on a generalized 3-simplex constraint: $f(x,y,z) = C \cdot \mathbf{1}_{x+y+z > \text{constant}}$.
* **Why?** In 1D, 2 points share a boundary. In 2D, the Voronoi/Delaunay tessellation is dictated by "empty circles" (or empty spheres in the projected 3D lattice space). An extreme gap in 2D is bounded by a Ford sphere. Tangency of Ford spheres requires at least 3 mutually tangent spheres to bound a "hole". Therefore, the minimum number of adjacent gaps that dictate an extreme event is 3. The density will exhibit strict support constraints, repelling large gaps from clustering together without a bridging micro-gap.

### 3. Cluster Universality: Cluster-3 Replaces Cluster-2
**Prediction:** The fundamental cluster unit will jump from **2 to 3**. 
* In 1D, the "Farey addition" (mediant) acts on a pair of points. In 2D, the fundamental building block is the Farey plane. A triangle of mutually visible lattice points forms the base of a pyramid. If you look at extreme gaps (the largest Voronoi cells), they will be "shielded" by a triplet of smaller cells, not a pair. This is geometrically forced by the dimension of the bounding facets in 3D lattice space. 

### 4. Extreme-Value Universality: Arithmetic (Gumbel/Poisson-like), not Tracy-Widom
**Prediction:** 2D Farey gaps will **not** follow Tracy-Widom. They will remain in the arithmetic universality class (which manifests as Gumbel or modified Poisson statistics).
* **Why?** Tracy-Widom arises from strongly correlated eigenvalue processes (like the edge of the spectrum of a random matrix) governed by the Airy kernel. Farey sequences are deterministic, arithmetic objects. While they exhibit local repulsion (due to the visible lattice constraint $\gcd=1$), their long-range rigidity is governed by the translation flows on $SL(3, \mathbb{Z}) \backslash SL(3, \mathbb{R})$. The extreme values of modular/arithmetical flows generally converge to the Fréchet or Gumbel distributions, bypassing random matrix universality entirely.

### 5. Practical Use: Why Raw 2D Farey fails QMC (and how to fix it)
**Prediction:** Standard 2D Farey points are **terrible** for standard Euclidean Quasi-Monte Carlo due to massive clustering. *However*, mapping the sequence via hyperbolic geometry creates a novel, highly competitive low-discrepancy sequence.
* Standard QMC relies on low discrepancy (Koksma-Hlawka inequality). 2D Farey is concentrated at the origin.
* **The Counter-Intuitive Bridge:** If you generate the 2D Farey points and map them using a bijection that mimics the Stern-Brocot tree in 2D (e.g., mapping mediants via hyperbolic rotations), you distribute the points evenly in hyperbolic space. If you then map this hyperbolic uniformity back to the Euclidean square using an inverse tangent or exponential map, you generate a new type of QMC sequence that has incredibly uniform angular distribution.
