### 1. The Statistical Takedown: Random Walks and Degrees of Freedom
The "rule" posits spikes at $Q = 2^a \cdot 5^5 \cdot m$. You have exactly four data points: $Q \in \{300k, 600k, 350k, 700k\}$. 

Let’s look at the structural reality of those points:
*   $300,000 = 3125 \times 3 \times 2^5$
*   $600,000 = 3125 \times 3 \times 2^6$
*   $350,000 = 3125 \times 7 \times 2^4$
*   $700,000 = 3125 \times 7 \times 2^5$

Notice that *every single one* of these $Q$ values is a highly "round" number—a multiple of $50,000$. The researchers didn't discover a rule; they reverse-engineered a formula that fit the few large, aesthetically pleasing multiples of $50k$ they decided to compute. 

With the degrees of freedom available (varying $a$, varying $m$, and setting the base to $5^5$), you can fit an elephant to four points. If the natural variation (the "smooth trend") drifts by $0.005$ across this range, observing excursions of $0.014 - 0.029$ in just 4 cherry-picked samples is entirely expected statistically. **This is noise masquerading as signal.**

### 2. The Mathematical Vacuum: Why 3 and 7?
In mathematics, if a rule works for a specific subset of integers (e.g., $m \in \{3, 7\}$), there is a **generative mechanism**. 
*   Is it modulo arithmetic? No. $3 \equiv 3 \pmod 4$ and $7 \equiv 3 \pmod 4$, but $11 \equiv 3 \pmod 4$ already broke the rule.
*   Is it quadratic residuosity? $\left(\frac{3}{5}\right) = -1$, $\left(\frac{7}{5}\right) = -1$, but $\left(\frac{11}{5}\right) = 1$. While this fits $\{3,7\}$ vs $\{11\}$, there is zero analytical reason why $NW(Q)$ would care about the Legendre symbol of $m$ modulo 5.
*   Without a theorem linking $NW(Q)$ to the factors of $Q$, the rule is pure numerology. The failure at $m=11$ isn't an exception; it's the universe telling you the pattern was a coincidence.

### 3. The $10^6$ Smoking Gun
The most damning piece of evidence is the evaluation at $Q = 10^6$.
$10^6 = 2^6 \cdot 5^6$. 
This explicitly **violates** the rule's condition that $Q$ must have $5^5$ and an $m$ *coprime* to 5. According to the rule, $10^6$ should yield a baseline value ($\sim 0.669$). 

Instead, it yielded $0.6793$. This is heavily elevated, and far closer to the "spikes" than to the baseline. 
**Conclusion:** The "spike" mechanism is not driven by $m \in \{3, 7\}$. It is driven merely by $Q$ being highly divisible by $2$s and $5$s. The rule misidentified the independent variable.

When algorithms compute sequences up to $Q$, numbers with massive factors of $2$ and $5$ (like $300k$, $500k$, $1000k$) often create boundary conditions in sieves, step-function jumps in memory allocation, or highly localized deviations in continuous approximation bounds. 
The "spikes" are not mathematically meaningful constants related to $NW$; they are likely localized oscillations caused by evaluating the function at heavily-smoothed boundaries.
