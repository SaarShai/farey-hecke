## 1. Verification of the Sym$^k$ Recurrence

Let $\alpha, \beta$ be the Satake parameters of $\Delta$ at a prime $p$, with $\alpha\beta = 1$. The Hecke eigenvalue for $\mathrm{Sym}^k\Delta$ is:

\[
\lambda_p(\mathrm{Sym}^k) = \sum_{j=0}^k \alpha^{k-j}\beta^j = \frac{\alpha^{k+1} - \beta^{k+1}}{\alpha - \beta} = U_k\!\left(\frac{\lambda_p}{2}\right),
\]

where $U_k$ is the $k$-th Chebyshev polynomial of the second kind, satisfying $U_{k+1}(x) = 2x\, U_k(x) - U_{k-1}(x)$. Substituting $x = \lambda_p/2$ yields:

\[
\lambda_p(\mathrm{Sym}^{k+1}) = \lambda_p \cdot \lambda_p(\mathrm{Sym}^k) - \lambda_p(\mathrm{Sym}^{k-1}),
\]

which is exactly the recurrence. The explicit formulas are:

| $k$ | $\lambda_p(\mathrm{Sym}^k)$ |
|-----|-----------------------------|
| 0 | $1$ |
| 1 | $\lambda_p$ |
| 2 | $\lambda_p^2 - 1$ |
| 3 | $\lambda_p^3 - 2\lambda_p$ |
| 4 | $\lambda_p^4 - 3\lambda_p^2 + 1$ |
| 5 | $\lambda_p^5 - 4\lambda_p^3 + 3\lambda_p$ |

All consistent with the user's formulas. ✓

## 4. Order of Vanishing at $s = 1/2$

The sign of the functional equation for $\mathrm{Sym}^k\Delta$ is given by:

\[
\epsilon(\mathrm{Sym}^k) = \epsilon(\Delta)^k \cdot (-1)^{k(k-1)/2},
\]

where $\epsilon(\Delta) = +1$ (since $\Delta$ is an even modular form). Thus:

\[
\epsilon(\mathrm{Sym}^k) = (-1)^{k(k-1)/2}.
\]

| $k$ | $k(k-1)/2$ | Sign $\epsilon$ | Predicted order of vanishing |
|-----|------------|-----------------|------------------------------|
| 1 | 0 | $+1$ | even (conjecturally 0) |
| 2 | 1 | $-1$ | odd (known to be ≥1; believed =1) |
| 3 | 3 | $-1$ | odd (believed =1) |
| 4 | 6 | $+1$ | even (believed =0) |
| 5 | 10 | $+1$ | even (believed =0) |
| 6 | 15 | $-1$ | odd |
| 7 | 21 | $-1$ | odd |
| 8 | 28 | $+1$ | even |

**Pattern:** For $k \equiv 0, 1 \pmod{4}$, the sign is $+1$ (even order); for $k \equiv 2, 3 \pmod{4}$, the sign is $-1$ (odd order). The sign does **not** simply alternate with parity of $k$; it follows the sequence $+, -, -, +, +, -, -, +, \ldots$ with period 4.

**Conjectures (not fully proven):**  
- $\mathrm{Sym}^2\Delta$ has a simple zero at the center (order 1).  
- $\mathrm{Sym}^3\Delta$ is believed to have a simple zero (order 1).  
- For $\mathrm{Sym}^4$ and $\mathrm{Sym}^5$, the order of vanishing is conjecturally 0.  

I am not aware of rigorous proofs for all these cases, but this is the expected pattern based on the sign and random matrix theory predictions.

## 5. Theoretical Limit on MUSIC as $k \to \infty$

The Hecke eigenvalues satisfy $|\lambda_p(\mathrm{Sym}^k)| \leq k+1$ (since $|U_k(x)| \leq k+1$ for $|x| \leq 1$). So the **signal-to-noise ratio (SNR)** degrades linearly with $k$.

**Theoretical limit with primes $\leq 10^5$:**

- The MUSIC algorithm requires enough coefficients to resolve the zeros. A rough rule of thumb: to resolve the first $n$ zeros, you need $\gtrsim 2n$ to $3n$ coefficients. With 9592 primes, you can resolve perhaps the first 3000–4000 zeros in principle.
- However, as $k$ increases, the variance of the coefficients increases (bounded by $(k+1)^2$), and the zeros of the L-function become more dense (the average spacing of zeros at height $T$ is $\sim \frac{2\pi}{\log T}$ for degree 1, but for degree $d$ it is $\sim \frac{2\pi}{d \log T}$? Actually, the density of zeros for a degree $d$ L-function is $\frac{d}{2\pi} \log\left(\frac{T}{2\pi e}\right)$ for large $T$, so the spacing decreases with $d$).
- For low-lying zeros (small $\gamma$), the spacing is roughly $\pi / \log N$ (with $N$ the conductor), so for $N=1$ they are not too dense.
- **Practical limit:** With primes up to $10^5$, I estimate that MUSIC can handle $\mathrm{Sym}^k\Delta$ reliably for $k \lesssim 15$ to $20$. Beyond that, the noise from the coefficient growth and the increased density of zeros (for higher zeros) will make it hard to resolve individual zeros. For the first 5 zeros, the limit is likely higher, maybe $k \lesssim 30$ or so.

**Summary:** The SNR degrades as $O(1/k)$, so there is no hard cutoff but a gradual loss of accuracy. With $10^5$ primes, $\mathrm{Sym}^{10}\Delta$ should still be manageable for low-lying zeros.
