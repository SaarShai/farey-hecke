Actually, one more critical point. The MUSIC algorithm requires forming a covariance matrix from the data and performing eigendecomposition. The signal subspace eigenvectors correspond to the signal, and the noise subspace to noise. MUSIC finds frequencies by looking for vectors orthogonal to the noise subspace.

For this to work well, you need:
1. Enough data points (large window)
2. Good SNR (signal-to-noise ratio)
3. Correct model order (number of signal components)
4. Stationarity of the signal

The explicit formula sum has infinitely many terms, so the "signal" is really infinite-dimensional. MUSIC will try to separate a finite number of "signal" components from "noise," where the "noise" includes all the higher zeros. This is a fundamental issue.

The practical question is: does the signal from low-lying zeros dominate enough that MUSIC can pick them out from the background of higher zeros? This is plausible because |x^ρ/ρ| = x^{β}/|ρ|, and for zeros with small β (close to the critical line, which they all are), this decays with |ρ|. But the number of zeros up to height T grows like T log T, so there are many of them.

For ψ(x) with x up to some X, the contribution of zeros up to height T dominates when T is roughly X or so. So to detect zeros up to height γ, you need data up to roughly e^γ. For the first 10 zeros of ζ (γ ≈ 14.13, 21.02, ...), you need primes up to roughly e^{25} ≈ 10^{10} or so, which is very manageable.

But the point is: the "noise" from higher zeros is structured, not random. MUSIC assumes noise is random (or at least, that signal and noise subspaces are orthogonal). This might not hold perfectly.
